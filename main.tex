\begin{abstract}
This experiment used the NI ELVIS III platform and a 2N3904 bipolar junction transistor to study the basic characteristics of a BJT and its use in switching and amplifier circuits. The work included observing transistor output characteristics, studying cutoff, saturation, and active-region behavior, measuring the DC bias point, voltage gain, and frequency response of a common-emitter amplifier, checking the voltage-following and current-gain behavior of an emitter follower, and completing a combined design task. The experiment involved breadboard construction, DC measurements, oscilloscope observation, and comparison between theoretical and measured values. The results were generally consistent with transistor theory. The common-emitter circuit showed clear voltage gain with phase inversion, the emitter follower had a voltage gain close to 1 with good current driving ability, and the final design task showed clear switching between different output states.

\subsection{Theoretical Background}
An NPN bipolar junction transistor has three terminals: base, emitter, and collector. In the active region, the base-emitter junction is forward biased and the collector-base junction is reverse biased. In this condition, the collector current is mainly controlled by the base current and can be described by the current gain $\beta$. A transistor usually works in cutoff, saturation, or active region, which correspond to off, fully on, and linear amplification.

A common-emitter amplifier needs a proper bias network to set a stable operating point, while coupling and bypass capacitors affect the frequency response. Its output is usually inverted and has noticeable voltage gain. An emitter follower gives an output voltage close to the input voltage, only lower by about one $V_{BE}$, so it has high input impedance, low output impedance, and a voltage gain close to 1. This makes it useful as a buffer stage.

\subsubsection{Transistor Amplifier}
In Section 3, a common-emitter amplifier was built. The bias resistors, collector resistor, emitter resistor, input coupling capacitor, and emitter bypass capacitor were connected according to the circuit diagram. After the circuit was built, an AC input signal was not applied immediately. Instead, the DC operating point was checked first. The base, collector, and emitter voltages were measured with the digital multimeter to confirm that the transistor was working in the active region. The collector voltage was checked carefully to see whether it was roughly in the middle of the supply range, since this allows a larger output swing and helps avoid early clipping.

\subsubsection{Frequency Response of the Amplifier}
After confirming the bias condition, a small sinusoidal signal was applied to the input through the coupling capacitor. The input and output waveforms were observed using the oscilloscope. The voltage gain was estimated by comparing the amplitudes, and the phase relation was checked because a common-emitter amplifier should normally invert the output. For the frequency response, the input frequency was changed gradually while keeping the signal small enough to avoid distortion, and the output amplitude at low, mid, and high frequencies was observed.

During testing, too many jumper wires were found to cause poor contact and reduce measurement reliability. Therefore, the wiring should be kept simple, and long unnecessary connections should be avoided. It was also important to prevent resistor and capacitor leads from touching each other or causing short circuits.

From the oscilloscope data, the input peak-to-peak voltage was about $201.9~\mathrm{mV}$ and the output peak-to-peak voltage was about $7.135~\mathrm{V}$, so the voltage gain was
\[
A_v=\frac{7.135}{0.2019}\approx 35.34.
\]
which gives, in decibels,
\[
20\log_{10}(35.34)\approx 30.96~\mathrm{dB}.
\]

From the Bode Analyzer result, the gain at about $100~\mathrm{Hz}$ was around $16.15~\mathrm{dB}$. Based on the small-signal model, the theoretical gain near $100~\mathrm{Hz}$ was about $34.2~\mathrm{dB}$, which is closer to the oscilloscope result but quite different from the Bode Analyzer reading. This difference was most likely caused by the Bode test amplitude, the selected measurement points, or the instrument settings. Overall, the common-emitter amplifier clearly produced voltage amplification and an inverted output.

\subsection{Section 5: Comprehensive Design Task}
Section 5 combines the amplification, coupling, and driving circuits from the previous sections. Instead of focusing on one stage only, this part looks at the output difference between the untriggered and triggered states to judge whether the whole circuit can perform the expected function in a stable way.

\subsubsection{Circuit Behaviour}
The idea of this circuit is that the front stage first amplifies the input signal, and then the signal is passed to the later stage through coupling so that the conduction state of the later stage can be controlled. In other words, the front stage provides enough signal amplitude, and the later stage decides whether a clear output will appear. So, in the untriggered state, the output should stay low, while in the triggered state, the output should change clearly.

\section{Conclusion}
This experiment studied the basic characteristics of a bipolar junction transistor, its switching use, and its amplifier applications. Sections 1 and 2 showed the output characteristics of the transistor and its cutoff and saturation behavior as a switch. The results were generally consistent with the expected theory.

In the amplifier part, the common-emitter circuit in Section 3 showed clear voltage gain and phase inversion, which means the circuit could amplify effectively. The estimated value of $\beta \approx 97.49$ was also close to the nominal value. The emitter follower in Section 4 showed a voltage gain close to 1, an in-phase input and output, and a noticeable current gain, which shows that it works well as a buffer stage.

Section 5 further showed that the front-stage amplifier, coupling network, and later driving stage could work together in one system and produce clearly different outputs in different states. Overall, the experiment agreed well with the theoretical BJT model. Although there were still effects from component tolerances and wiring conditions, the main results were clear and easy to observe.
